CASE FILE #001

PlayTime (1967)

TSPDT RANKING: #45 | VERDICT: ★



[ ENGLISH ]    [ ESPAÑOL ]



There are movies we watch, and others that reach our eyes only after passing through far too many other people's.

PlayTime unquestionably belongs to the second category. Before the projector even starts rolling, the movie arrives escorted by a fan club with academic credentials, ready to remind you—just in case you're an ignoramus—that it occupies the No. 45 spot on the They Shoot Pictures, Don't They? list.

You know you're about to consume a Masterpiece™. And that changes everything. With every endless static shot, we switch on our hidden-genius detector; with every absurd decision, we suspect some profundity we simply haven't managed to grasp yet; and whenever boredom reigns supreme, we flagellate ourselves with the thought that maybe we're the problem: perhaps we're simply not cultured enough to get the joke.

Which brings us to the sacrilege with which we inaugurate this blog:

If nobody had gone to the trouble of explaining to me that this was a masterpiece, would my humble brain ever have reached that conclusion on its own?

Relax, guardians of the canon. Put down the torches. We're not here to proclaim that international film criticism is a pyramid scheme or that critical prestige is one enormous collective fraud… Nor are we here to declare a movie bad or an unbearable slog simply because it bored us senseless. Our proposal is considerably more modest: give the myth a two-hour leave of absence and watch the footage with the unclouded eyes of someone who owes nobody anything.

So let's start dissecting No. 45.

And one thing has to be acknowledged from the very first frame:

PlayTime is exquisite.

Perhaps so exquisite it becomes cloying.



THE PERFECT MODEL

Let's start with what's difficult to dispute: PlayTime is visually impeccable. Every composition looks as though it was designed with a ruler, a set square, and some obsessive disorder yet to be classified. Bodies, furniture, doors, reflections, lines on the floor: everything occupies exactly the place Tati decided it should. He doesn't observe chaos; he painstakingly constructs every screw in the machinery of chaos and then pretends he just stumbled upon it.

And you can tell.

Boy, can you tell.

The artifice works because the places the movie moves through—airports, offices, hotels, tourist restaurants—already resemble models in real life: geometric, impersonal spaces where we could wake up in Paris or New York and take a while to notice the difference. Recognizable Paris barely survives in the occasional fleeting reflection in a pane of glass. And yet Tati criticizes artifice by constructing one of the most monumental artifices in film history. One that cost, to be precise, 17 million French francs (roughly $3.4 million at the time). Because to shoot PlayTime, Tati decided Paris wasn't Paris enough and built his own: Tativille, a small city of glass, steel, and concrete, complete with buildings, roads, and even its own power plant.

Tati's perfectionism reached such delirious heights that, after spending the equivalent of a small country's GDP on building his own concrete Paris, he ran out of money for the cheapest part: human beings. In a display of austerity worthy of the finest bureaucratic comedy, the director decided that half the occupants of his offices didn't really need a third dimension. He filled the backgrounds with life-size photographic cutouts, while flesh-and-blood extras wandered around in front of them to complete the illusion.

There is something admirably insane about all this. And something deliciously obscene about the satire.

The model is extraordinary.

The question is how long we want to spend inside it.



OKAY, JACQUES, WE GET IT

The problem with PlayTime isn't that its game is bad. The problem is that forty minutes in, we already know how to play.

Spot the tiny accident hidden somewhere in the frame. Wait for an object to make an unexpected noise. Watch a character discreetly wrestle with a door, an armchair, or some marvel of modern life supposedly designed to make things easier. Discover that while we were watching one thing, three others were happening in the background.

It's clever. Sometimes extremely so.

But there are still nearly two hours left.

And this is where PlayTime starts to feel less like a movie to me than a succession of sketches performed by a director incapable of leaving a party while there's still one gag left to serve. The precision remains admirable; what diminishes is the surprise.

The Royal Garden pushes this logic into delirium. The restaurant opens before it is actually finished and begins disintegrating before our eyes: the floor breaks, the decor falls apart, the waiters improvise, the customers invade spaces that shouldn't exist, and every disaster gives birth to the next. It's a monumental choreography of chaos and probably the definitive demonstration of Tati's ability to control dozens of tiny actions simultaneously.

It also seems to go on forever.

And here we arrive at one of those uncomfortable questions that tend to go down badly whenever we're talking about a Masterpiece™: does the fact that something is incredibly difficult to do necessarily mean we need to watch it for quite so long?

There's an old Spanish saying: "Good things, when brief, are twice as good." Tati appears to disagree. PlayTime is generous to the point of exhaustion. The movie fills every shot with ideas, details, and little gifts for the attentive viewer. The problem with gifts is that, after opening the thirty-seventh one, you start appreciating the thought more than the contents.

Two hours of virtuosity are still two hours. But perhaps all this analysis is unnecessary. After decades of critical literature devoted to PlayTime, I found a comment beneath the movie on Filmin from a user named Yolanda who settled the matter in six words: "How awful. A lesson in pretentiousness."

Film criticism had finally achieved its perfect form.



AND WHAT'S UNDERNEATH THE MODEL?

At this point, it would be unfair to say that PlayTime has nothing to say. It does, and quite clearly.

Tati's world is modern, efficient, international, and perfectly interchangeable. Airports, offices, hotels, storefronts: Paris could be any other city because modernity has accomplished the minor miracle of making them all start to look alike. American tourists search for Paris while moving through spaces where barely a trace of Paris remains; the monuments survive as reflections in glass doors, almost ghosts of a city that exists somewhere beyond the model.

Technology doesn't come off particularly well, either. Everything promises comfort, efficiency, and progress until a human being shows up and tries to use it. Then come the impossible doors, the absurd furniture, the unnecessary gadgets, and that succession of minor battles between people and objects that fuels so much of the comedy.

The idea works. It even feels somewhat prophetic: nearly sixty years later, the homogenization of cities, tourism turned into a product, and our fascination with technological solutions to problems we may never have had hardly feel like archaeological curiosities.

But this is where my problem with PlayTime returns: the sophistication of its form far exceeds the sophistication of what it has to say.

The characters are disoriented within the architecture. We are disoriented within the frame. In other words, the form forces us to experience that disorientation ourselves. Fine. Tati gives us the freedom to look—but what reward do I get for exercising that freedom for two hours?

Are we confusing perfect packaging with a profound film?

Tati needs a city built specifically for the movie, fiendishly complicated choreography, dozens of simultaneous actions, reflections, sounds, glass, extras, and two hours of obsessive precision to tell us that modernity makes us uniform, that technology can complicate what it was meant to simplify, and that underneath all that order we remain fairly chaotic creatures. Or that when design fails, the party begins.

Those aren't bad ideas. They aren't even minor ones.

But faced with such a magnificent formal cathedral, I expected to find something more on the altar.



VERDICT

So let's return to the original question: if nobody had told me that PlayTime was a masterpiece, would I have reached that conclusion on my own?

No.

I would have thought I was watching a movie of extraordinary visual intelligence, technically dazzling and constructed with almost pathological precision. I would also have thought that someone should have taken an hour of footage away from Jacques Tati and handed him a pair of scissors.

I understand why PlayTime matters. I understand what it does, how it does it, and why it has fascinated critics and filmmakers for decades. The problem is that understanding a movie does not oblige me to admire it to the officially prescribed degree.

No. 45 among the greatest movies ever made?

The good-taste police are going to have to come and get us.

★
